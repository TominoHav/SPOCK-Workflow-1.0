# Account switching: what the market shows

Ten cards cover a single market reality: every guided switching service in this set executes the same transfer and then hands the card-on-file problem back to the customer. The Czech market has not moved above the PAD statutory floor in any externally visible way. The UK market demonstrates what movement above it looks like, and the most consequential version is not transparency of the process, though one incumbent has built that; it is treating salary arrival as the event that confirms the switch succeeded and building a product around that moment. No Czech bank has done either. The gap between what the market provides and what the five promoted opportunities require is not a documentation gap; it is a product gap the market has chosen not to close.

[rec:A7-r01]

## What switching services cover

All nine guided services transfer the same things and exclude the same thing. The core scope is set by statute in the Czech market and by scheme rules in the UK, and the result is identical: direct debits, standing orders, and account balance move automatically; card-on-file merchant credentials do not. No bank in this set has changed that structure. The only participant where card-on-file payments are handled by the same method as everything else is the self-managed switch, where the customer manages every payment type individually and nothing transfers automatically.

**[universal] ST1** Nine of ten guided switching services automatically transfer standing orders, direct debits, and the existing account balance, and redirect incoming payments from the closed old account for a defined minimum period.
All nine bank-managed services (CSOB, Komercni banka, Air Bank, Moneta, Starling, Monzo, Revolut, NatWest, Lloyds) cover this scope as a baseline. The UK scheme (CASS) redirects incoming payments for at least 36 months; the Czech PAD minimum is 13 months. This standard partially addresses O3 in that the mechanics of the switch are automated, removing most of the customer action required; it partially addresses O4 in that completion of the transfer is a defined event. What it does not address is whether the customer can see the process while it runs, or whether the completion event is the right one to count.

**[universal] ST2** Card-on-file merchant credentials are excluded from all guided switching schemes by statute or scheme rule, confirmed by every participant in this set, with no bank in this set having deployed a tool to identify or migrate them.
Ten of ten participants confirm this gap. NatWest names it on its switch page with Amazon as the example merchant. Starling's switching documentation confirms that customers must update card details stored by merchants in the same way as they would if their card expired. CSOB, Komercni banka, Air Bank and Moneta carry the same exclusion by operation of PAD Article 10. The self-managed switch carries it too, though without the direct debit and standing order automation that reduces the manual burden elsewhere. This is the structural root of O1 and O6: the gap that makes it impossible for any guided service in this set to confirm the old account is clear of obligations.

**[universal] The old account closes automatically on the completion date of every guided switching service in this set, eliminating the customer's visibility of any payment still pointing at it.**
Nine of ten guided services close the old account as part of the process. This is a feature of the scheme, not a defect: incoming payment redirection covers the most common residual payment types (direct debits, standing orders) for at least 13 months. What it does not cover is card-on-file subscriptions, which neither redirect nor trigger a failure notification on the new account. Once the old account closes, no participant in this set gives the customer a view of what is still arriving there. This directly defines the problem behind O6: the old account stays open because no bank offers an alternative that replaces that visibility.

**[rare] P4** The self-managed approach is the only method in this set where the old account stays open throughout the transition, giving the customer a live view of every payment still pointing at it before they decide to close.
One of ten. The self-managed switch keeps both accounts running simultaneously. The customer sees every stray payment before it becomes a problem. Which?, MoneySavingExpert and GoCompare all confirm that maintaining this visibility is the primary reason customers choose the manual approach over a guided one. This is the comparison every guided service in this set implicitly competes against for O1 and O6: the manual route that answers the visibility question, at the cost of the customer managing every payment type individually with no automation, no scheme guarantee and no defined completion date.

**[emerging] ST5** Salary-moment features that activate on first salary receipt after a switch are deployed by two participants (Monzo and Revolut), both marked innovator, making salary arrival the operational definition of a completed switch.
Two of ten. Monzo's Salary Sorter allocates incoming salary across named Pots on arrival; Get Paid Early releases the payment 24 hours before standard clearing. Revolut's Early Salary operates the same advance-release mechanism. Both features require a CASS switch to activate. The two are independent designs reaching the same conclusion: the first salary in the new account is when the switch is done. This is the UK market's answer to O2 (salary timing) and to O4 (what complete means): not a better completion metric for the bank's reporting, but a product event the customer experiences.

## Who the market serves

All nine guided services target adult retail current-account holders with no income minimum and no minimum deposit. Below that floor, the set splits. UK neobanks target digitally fluent customers who expect app-only service and no branch interaction. UK and Czech incumbents split the customer base by product tier rather than by digital behaviour, with a free base tier for the general population and a paid tier for higher-income or higher-activity customers. Czech banks target domestic customers almost exclusively, with Komercni banka as the only Czech participant to publish an English-language switching page.

**[universal] All nine guided switching services are open to adult retail customers with no income floor, no minimum balance, and no minimum deposit required to participate.**
Nine of ten. The self-managed switch has no eligibility requirement at all. This finding bears on none of the promoted opportunities directly; it establishes that no switching service in this set excludes the segment this run is addressing.

**[common] Seven of ten participants operate a free base current account tier with no qualifying conditions, alongside paid tiers carrying additional benefits.**
Seven of ten: Starling (free personal account), Monzo (free tier), Revolut (Standard), NatWest (Select Account), Lloyds (Classic Account), Air Bank (0 CZK, no conditions), Moneta (Ucet Tom Plus, 0 CZK). CSOB's Plus Konto is also free with no qualifying conditions, bringing the count to eight if confirmed. Komercni banka's Standard plan at CZK 39/month with a fee waiver for those aged 6 to 26 is the closest exception. This bears on none of the promoted opportunities directly; a free base tier removes the cost barrier to opening a new account before a switch, but cost is not what the five opportunities are about.

**[common] Six of ten bank participants maintain a branch or in-person service channel alongside digital switching: CSOB (280 branches and 3,300 Czech Post points), Komercni banka (branches), Air Bank (32 branches), Moneta (100-plus branches), NatWest (branches, declining from 2026), and Lloyds Bank (700-plus branches, plus Halifax and Bank of Scotland under a shared access policy from 2025).**
Six of ten bank participants. Starling, Monzo and Revolut operate with no branch network. The self-managed switch has no bank on the new-account side that this card profiles. Branch access is relevant to the switching customer who wants in-person assistance during a complex move, but none of the branch-maintaining participants in this set offers a specific switching service through that channel that is documented above the PAD or CASS floor. This bears on none of the promoted opportunities directly.

**[emerging] P3** Three Czech banks publish no English-language switching documentation of any kind, making their switching service inaccessible to any customer who relies on English-language material to understand what the service covers.
Three of ten: CSOB, Air Bank and Moneta carry no English-language switching page, scope statement or timeline in any accessible source. Komercni banka publishes an English switching page naming what transfers and what the customer must still do, making it the Czech benchmark for transparency, not a gold standard. NatWest's Day 1 to Day 6-7 schedule defines the UK benchmark. The distance between the two is the measurable gap O3 is asking this initiative to close. This finding bears on O3 (a customer who cannot read the documentation cannot track the request) and indirectly on O4 (a bank that does not describe completion cannot be held to one).

## Journey capabilities

This is where the ten cards separate most clearly. The statutory floor covers payment migration. What sits above it, and whether anything does, is entirely a product choice. UK neobanks have made that choice visibly and in the same direction: salary arrival as the completion signal, app visibility of what has transferred, and in two cases a mechanism to reduce the card-on-file credential count before the switch starts. Czech banks have made no visible choice above the floor. That absence is not a cost constraint; Moneta's real-time marketing infrastructure and Air Bank's investment integration show the capability exists. It is a product scope decision.

**[universal] ST2** Card-on-file merchant credentials require manual customer action after any switch, under any approach, with no participant in this set having deployed a tool to automate the update.
Ten of ten confirm this. Starling's switching documentation states customers must update card details stored by merchants the same way they would if their card expired. NatWest's switch page names this explicitly and gives Amazon as the example. The self-managed switch card confirms it as a structural limitation: no bank, no scheme, and no switching service in any market covered by this research has the ability to retrieve or surface a list of merchants holding a customer's old card details. Card credentials are stored by merchants against a card number, and the bank is not a party to that relationship. This is the root cause behind O1 and the structural barrier behind O6. It is confirmed as universal not because every bank chose it but because the payment scheme architecture makes it inescapable for every participant without external tooling.

**[emerging] ST4** Virtual cards linked to named sub-accounts or subscription categories reduce card-on-file exposure before a switch and are available at three participants, all marked innovator: Starling Bank (up to five virtual cards, each linked to a named Saving Space, free on all accounts), Monzo (virtual cards on paid plans from £3/month), and Revolut (virtual cards available, creatable and deletable in-app).
Three of ten. The mechanism is the same across all three: a customer who routes subscriptions through a virtual card before switching has fewer merchant-held card numbers tied to their main account card. When the main card number changes at switch, the virtual card number is unaffected, and merchants attached to it require no credential update. Starling's implementation is the strongest in the set: virtual cards are free, available on all accounts, and the Saving Spaces integration makes the card-subscription separation visible before any switch is initiated. Monzo and Revolut offer the same mechanism with less structural integration. None of the three contacts merchants on the customer's behalf; all three require deliberate setup before the switch, not after. The capability is an O1 partial answer. A customer who set up virtual cards does not face the full card-on-file problem on switching; a customer who did not faces it unchanged.

**[emerging] In-progress switching status, showing which direct debits and standing orders have transferred during the 7-day window, is visible inside the app for three UK neobanks and via a digital assistant for one UK incumbent: Starling (in-app), Monzo (in-app), Revolut (in-app) and NatWest (Cora, which confirms direct debit transfers on request).**
Four of ten, three of them innovators. The neobank implementations surface transfer status through the same notification and activity feed the customer uses for transactions; there is no separate switching dashboard. NatWest's Cora covers direct debit confirmation and nothing else. No Czech bank has an equivalent in English-language accessible material. This is an O3 finding: the request does not go entirely quiet for customers at these four banks, though what is visible is limited to direct debits and standing orders. Card-on-file transfer status is invisible everywhere, because nothing transfers.

**[common] ST3** Named savings sub-accounts or pots within the main current account, without a separate product application, are available at seven of ten participants and function as the structural prerequisite for salary-moment features.
Seven of ten: Starling (Saving Spaces), Monzo (Pots), Revolut (Vaults), Komercni banka (10 saving envelopes), Air Bank (10 simultaneous accounts), Moneta (savings envelopes) and Lloyds Bank (Club Lloyds Monthly Saver). CSOB and NatWest have no equivalent documented at this level; the self-managed switch has no destination account this card profiles. At seven of ten this is above the majority threshold for common by C87 arithmetic (70%), making it a common market standard. The sub-accounts matter for O4 specifically because Monzo's Salary Sorter, the only confirmed market answer to the "complete means the form was worked" problem, requires Pots to exist before the first salary lands. A new account that lacks sub-account capability cannot replicate that answer. This does not mean sub-accounts alone address O4; they do not.

**[rare] P7** A published switching timeline that commits the bank to a specific outcome on a specific day is offered by one participant: NatWest, which names Days 1, 2, 3 and 6-7 of the CASS process on its switch page.
One of ten. NatWest's Day 1 through Day 6-7 breakdown commits: Day 1-2 the customer chooses a switch date and the old bank is notified, Day 3 the new NatWest account is established, Days 6-7 the balance transfers and the old account closes. No other participant in this set publishes a schedule at this level of specificity. Czech banks publish no switching timeline. UK neobanks surface in-progress status but do not publish the schedule before the switch begins. Komercni banka names what transfers and what the customer must do, but gives no timeline. NatWest's schedule is the closest the set gets to what O3 requires: a request that carries state while it is in flight. It covers only the bank-managed mechanics; card-on-file subscriptions have no day on NatWest's schedule because they are not part of the scheme.

## What sets participants apart

The set divides into three positions. UK innovators differentiate on product features built around the switching event: salary-moment activation, virtual card credential separation, and app-visible transfer status. UK incumbents differentiate on scale, branch access and cash incentives, without building above the journey floor. Czech banks differentiate on CX, pricing or platform depth, none of which reaches the switching journey in any accessible public material. The single most important USP in the set for this run is one that almost nobody has deployed: explicit, pre-switch communication of what the service does not cover.

**[emerging] ST6** Two participants (NatWest and Starling Bank) explicitly state in publicly accessible switching documentation that card-linked subscription payments fall outside the scheme's scope and that the customer must update them manually.
Two of ten. NatWest names the exclusion on its switch page, uses the phrase "regular debit card payments," gives Amazon as a named example merchant, and says the customer must handle it "in the same way as you would now if your card expired." Starling's switching help page confirms the same exclusion in similar terms. No Czech bank and no other UK participant in this set matches this level of explicit pre-switch disclosure. For O1, this is the market's only approach to the gap that falls short of solving it: naming the problem rather than addressing it, which sets customer expectations before the switch rather than letting them discover the problem after the old account has closed.

**[emerging] ST5** Salary-moment activation is the most consequential differentiator for attracting a main-banking relationship, and two UK neobanks (Monzo and Revolut) have built it while no Czech bank or UK incumbent has.
Two of ten, both innovators. Monzo's Salary Sorter and Get Paid Early are specifically designed to fire when the first salary arrives, giving the customer a product interaction at the moment the new account becomes their financial home. Revolut's Early Salary provides the same advance-receipt mechanism following its UK banking licence in March 2026. These are not switching features that assist with the move; they are retention features that confirm the move is complete. For O2 and O4, the market has one answer and it is held by two innovators. The Czech market has no equivalent.

**[rare] CSOB's 3,300 Czech Post agency points constitute a physical distribution advantage no other Czech bank in this set can match.**
One of ten. No Czech bank outside CSOB has a comparable arrangement with a national infrastructure partner. For switching customers who need in-person assistance or document verification at a counter, CSOB's physical reach is unmatched. This bears on none of the five promoted opportunities directly; the promoted opportunities are about what happens during and after a digital or semi-digital switching process, not about branch access.

**[rare] Air Bank has won the KPMG Customer Bank of the Year award for nine consecutive years, the only confirmed CX recognition of this duration and independence in the Czech market.**
One of ten. KPMG's CX Top 100 Brands ranking is independently administered. Nine consecutive wins establish Air Bank as the Czech sustained CX leader. No other Czech bank in this set has published comparable independent recognition. This bears on none of the five promoted opportunities directly; CX awards measure the day-to-day experience and do not assess the switching journey, where Air Bank's primary site returned HTTP 404 on the research date and no English-language documentation of its switching service exists.

## Pricing and incentives

The pricing picture across ten cards divides on two axes: the monthly cost of the base account and whether the bank deploys a financial incentive to attract a switch. Both are well-evidenced. Neither is a response to the opportunities this run is working on. The pricing signal that matters for this run is not the cost of the product; it is what UK incumbents' escalating cash incentives reveal about their switching economics.

**[common] A free or zero-fee base current account with no qualifying conditions is the market norm, available at eight of ten participants including all five Czech banks.**
Eight of ten (treating CSOB's Plus Konto, which requires no qualifying conditions, as confirmed, though at reduced confidence given the inaccessible primary site): Air Bank, Moneta, CSOB, Starling, Monzo, Revolut (Standard), NatWest (Select), Lloyds (Classic). Komercni banka's Standard plan costs CZK 39/month with a fee waiver for customers aged 6 to 26, the only base account in the set with a maintenance cost that is not waivable on activity. The self-managed switch incurs no switching cost but may carry continued fees on the old account during the transition period. This finding bears on none of the five promoted opportunities directly; the absence of a cost barrier to opening a new account is a precondition for any switching proposition, not a differentiator within it.

**[emerging] P2** Cash switching incentives, paid on completion of a guided switch with qualifying conditions, are offered by two UK incumbents (NatWest at £200 and Lloyds at £200-£500) and by no participant in the Czech market.
Two of ten (three if Starling's non-cash toothbrush offer is included, giving a count of three of ten). NatWest ran a £200 cash offer in 2026, after a £150 offer earlier in the same year. Lloyds ran a £250 campaign in January 2026, followed by £200-£500 in February to April 2026. Both set qualifying conditions on salary deposit and direct debit count. No Czech bank publishes any equivalent switching incentive. This bears on none of the five promoted opportunities directly; cash incentives address the question of whether a customer switches, not the question of whether the switch succeeds or how the customer experiences it.

**[emerging] A paid plan tier converting the monthly fee into a named lifestyle benefit is used by two participants (Lloyds Bank Club Lloyds and Monzo Perks) to reframe cost as a product choice.**
Two of ten. Lloyds Club Lloyds charges £3/month (some sources cite £5 for 2026) with a fee waived on monthly deposits of £2,000 or more, and gives the customer an annual choice between a Disney+ subscription, cinema tickets, a coffee club membership or a magazine subscription. Monzo Perks at £7-£9/month adds a Railcard, weekly Greggs snacks, Uber One and YouTube Premium Lite. The mechanism reframes the fee as a product with a variable return. This bears on none of the five promoted opportunities directly.

**[common] Savings interest above 2% AER is available within the main account relationship at seven of ten participants, reducing the financial cost of holding money in the new account during a transition.**
Seven of ten: Starling (Saving Spaces at 4.5% AER), Monzo (Instant Access savings at 3.0-3.25%), Revolut (variable savings vaults), Lloyds (Club Lloyds Monthly Saver at 6.25% AER on £25-£400/month), Air Bank (4.70% instant-access fixed deposit, 2.60% savings account), Moneta (savings account at 3.50% CZK), Komercni banka (interest on savings envelopes). CSOB and NatWest have savings products but no rate above 2% was confirmed in English-language accessible material; the self-managed switch's old bank may or may not pay interest on the maintained float. This bears on none of the five promoted opportunities directly.

## Above-floor design

The floor is PAD in the Czech market and CASS in the UK. Above it, the set produces two directions: UK innovators have designed product features for the switching moment, and Czech innovators have designed product breadth for the banking relationship. The switching moment and the banking relationship are the same product space; the difference is where the investment landed. Moneta's real-time marketing infrastructure responds to a transaction within 30 seconds; it has no confirmed switching journey application. Air Bank's Portu investment integration sets it apart from every Czech incumbent; its primary site returned 404 on 26 August 2026. The Czech market's digital investment is real and is pointed at the wrong problem for this run.

**[emerging] P1** Three UK innovators have built product features that activate during or immediately after the switch, treating the transition itself as a product design surface: Starling (virtual cards reducing pre-switch card-on-file exposure), Monzo (Salary Sorter and Get Paid Early activating on first salary), and Revolut (Early Salary post-banking licence).
Three of ten, all innovators. No UK incumbent and no Czech bank has built anything equivalent. The common thread is designing for the switching event rather than around it. Starling's design is pre-switch: isolate credentials before moving. Monzo's and Revolut's designs are post-switch: confirm the move by receiving salary. These are different moments and different problems, but both treat the switching event as a design opportunity that the statutory floor ignores. For O1, O2 and O4, the UK innovators are the only market participants who have produced something that bears on any of them as a product, not just as a confirmed gap.

**[emerging] TR-4** Two Czech banks have deployed real-time or near-real-time transaction infrastructure that could support switching journey features: Moneta (AWS/SAS marketing engine responding within 30 seconds of a transaction, in production April 2026) and CSOB (Camunda BPM process automation and Kyndryl partnership from June 2026 targeting operational efficiency for 4 million customers).
Two of ten. Neither has confirmed a switching journey application for this infrastructure. Moneta's system is documented as a marketing execution tool; it fires offers in response to transactions, not confirmations or status updates in response to a switch event. CSOB's transformation is described in terms of process efficiency and customer experience improvement without naming the switching journey. The capability is present and the switching application is absent. This bears on O3 and O4 in the sense that the infrastructure exists to build against; it does not address either opportunity in any confirmed product today.

**[emerging] Two Czech banks (Komercni banka and Air Bank) and one UK neobank (Monzo) explicitly treat switching volume as a growth metric, either through public disclosure or through CX award positioning that implies competitive switching activity.**
Three of ten (two Czech, one UK). Monzo is the only participant in the set to publish net CASS gain data by quarter (9,934 net gains in Q3 2025). Komercni banka's English-language switching page and its completed Temenos migration both position switching capability as a deliberate offering to the market. Air Bank's nine consecutive KPMG CX awards and 49% customer growth 2022-2025 indicate it is attracting customers from somewhere. None of the three documents what happens to a switcher after the guided service concludes. This bears on none of the five promoted opportunities directly; it establishes that the competitive market for switching volume is active in both countries.

**[rare] One participant has built an infrastructure-layer switching capability that no bank in this set can replicate as a product investment: CSOB's 3,300 Czech Post agency points as a physical switching service channel, making it the counterparty in most Czech switches and the hardest destination to displace.**
One of ten. CSOB's Czech Post arrangement is structural and long-term; it cannot be replicated by a digital challenger through technology spending. For any Czech switching scenario, CSOB is the most probable origin or destination. Its decisions on the switching journey, including any choice to add status tracking or post-switch visibility, affect more customers than the equivalent decision at any other Czech bank. This bears on none of the five promoted opportunities directly, but it defines the competitive position any new switching offering must improve against.

## Verdict

### Diagnosis

The Czech switching market runs at the PAD statutory floor, where guided services complete the payment migration and then hand both the card-on-file problem and the silence of an in-flight request back to the customer.

### Parity

Two standards define what omitting creates a visible deficiency. Automatic transfer of standing orders, direct debits and the account balance, plus incoming payment redirection, is universal (9 of 10 guided services): a bank that does not offer this is offering the customer less protection than every other institution in the set. Savings sub-accounts within the main account without a separate product are common (7 of 10): a bank that lacks them cannot replicate the salary-sorter mechanism the UK market has already built as the answer to O4 and O2. A free base account is common (8 of 10): charging for the base tier in the Czech market now requires explanation. Acknowledging the card-on-file exclusion explicitly, which only two participants do, is not yet parity; it is an opening.

### The opening

Three emerging standards sit where no Czech bank operates and where the market has not set a firm expectation. Virtual cards for pre-switch credential separation (3 of 10, all innovators) are available but not expected; the customer who has not set them up before switching faces the full card-on-file problem and is not told to. Salary-moment features (2 of 10, both UK innovators) are the only confirmed market answer to O2 and the only product event that makes O4 real for the customer: the Czech market has no product in this space. Explicit disclosure of the card-on-file exclusion (2 of 10, UK only) addresses O1 at the information level without solving it. The gap between these three emerging standards and the Czech PAD floor is the space the five promoted opportunities describe. The pattern in the absences is that the complete answer, a tool that shows the customer what is still attached to the old account after it closes, does not exist anywhere in this set.

### Risk

Three risks are evidenced by the cards. CSOB's 3,300 Czech Post agency points and 4 million customers make it the counterparty in most Czech switching scenarios; it has the margin to absorb above-floor investment pressure and the data volume to learn from switching behaviour faster than any other Czech bank. The self-managed switch is the direct competitive substitute for every guided service on the one capability that matters most for O1 and O6: it keeps the old account open, and a customer who values that visibility over convenience will choose it, paying with two to four hours of manual work and up to 90 days of dual-account management. Third: the card-credential migration tooling that would make the card-on-file problem solvable (Galileo Payment Switch, Visa Enhanced Subscription Manager) is commercially available and undeployed by any participant in this set. The first bank to integrate it changes what O1 looks like entirely, and this initiative's solutions will be evaluated against that product once it exists.
