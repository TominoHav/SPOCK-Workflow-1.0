## What they claim sets them apart

The cards show USP claims in three main patterns: consumer-facing tools for subscription or direct debit visibility and control, digital-first process leadership for standing orders, and institutional scale or penetration. Two participants fall outside all three: tapix.io as a B2B infrastructure layer and substitute:contact-centre-call as a legally grounded channel rather than a product. No claim of a unified recurring commitment view confirmed from primary documentation appears anywhere in the cohort.

**[universal]** No participant offers a confirmed unified recurring commitment view spanning standing orders, direct debits and card subscriptions in a single consumer screen, and the only participant that approaches the claim cannot have it verified. 10 of 11 participants either make no such claim or explicitly list its absence in their confirmed scope. revolut.com's Smart Subscriptions is described as "full visibility of all subscriptions, direct debits and recurring payments in one place," but revolut.com and help.revolut.com returned HTTP 403 on all access attempts during research, making the detailed scope unconfirmable from Revolut's own documentation. lloydsbank.com and natwest.com each confirm in their own stated scope that their subscription features cover debit card payments only and that standing orders and direct debits are managed separately, with no unified view across all payment types. The remaining 8 of 11 make no unified view claim of any kind. This finding is the market expression of H1: a product that resolves the gap CS identified is not available in a confirmed form from any participant in this cohort.

**[common]** Eight of 11 participants claim customer-facing control over at least one recurring payment type as a differentiator, but each addresses a different commitment type and the claims do not add up to coverage of all three. kb.cz and airbank.cz claim management of standing orders (bank-initiated credit transfers). revolut.com, lloydsbank.com and natwest.com claim visibility and payment control for card subscriptions. monzo.com claims in-app cancellation of direct debit mandates (UK BACS only) and proactive card controls via virtual cards with auto-delete. n26.com claims standing order lifecycle management entirely in the mobile app. substitute:contact-centre-call claims the legal right to cancel any Continuous Payment Authority on the customer's behalf. csob.cz (no recurring payment management feature confirmed across two research runs), moneta.cz (USP claims are digital penetration and real-time personalization, not payment control), and tapix.io (B2B only, no consumer-facing product) fall outside this group. The fragmentation bears on H1 (no unified view across all commitment types) and H4 (process and capability differ by commitment type, with clients not told which applies before they make contact).

**[emerging]** Three of 11 participants name subscription management as a dedicated consumer product, but all three confirm the same ceiling: they stop at payment blocking or merchant signposting for card subscriptions, not contract cancellation. revolut.com's Smart Subscriptions allows the customer to block a specific merchant or subscription amount; it does not cancel the underlying subscription mandate, and the cards state that cancellation requires contacting the merchant directly. lloydsbank.com's Manage Subscriptions (built with Minna Technologies since July 2020) initiates a cancellation flow but requires the customer to contact the service provider to terminate the contract; the card confirms that the app "blocks further payments" while directing customers elsewhere, and that a cancellation once initiated in the app cannot be reversed without calling the bank. natwest.com's Subscriptions feature links customers to merchant cancellation pages rather than cancelling the payment from the bank side, and natwest.com's own feature description states explicitly that stopping a payment does not end the underlying contract. The evidence basis for revolut.com and lloydsbank.com is reduced: their primary documentation was inaccessible during research (HTTP 403 and HTTP errors respectively), and their scope is drawn from secondary sources. natwest.com's feature is confirmed from primary documentation (S-natwest-sub-page). All three are either unavailable in or not operating primarily in the Czech Republic: Revolut holds a Czech banking licence but primary confirmation of Smart Subscriptions' scope is absent; Lloyds and NatWest are UK-only. This bearing on H2 is direct: the product category that has emerged to address card subscription control does not resolve the gap H2 names, because blocking a payment and cancelling a card-on-file authority are distinct acts in every deployment confirmed in this cohort.

**[emerging]** Three of 11 participants claim standing order digital-first process or documentation leadership as their primary differentiator, and none of the three makes any card subscription management claim alongside it. kb.cz was the first Czech incumbent to confirm a digital-only standing order channel, eliminating paper instructions at branches as of 1 January 2024, confirmed in KB's own customer communications (S-kb-cards-page, S-kb-sipo-faq); its FAQ is the most explicit documentation of recurring payment types among Czech incumbents, covering standing orders, SIPO and direct debit in a single resource. airbank.cz claims the most detailed standing order self-service guide among Czech incumbents in this cohort, with a documented create-edit-pause-cancel lifecycle and step-by-step screenshots in its own help pages, independently rated by 5nej.cz and pickthebank.eu for ease of recurring payment management (S-airbank-trvaly-help, S-airbank-ib-standing, S-airbank-5nej, S-airbank-pickthebank). n26.com claims the most explicitly documented standing order lifecycle among the neobanks in this cohort, with a dedicated support article covering creation, editing and deletion from primary documentation (S-n26-standing-orders); N26 is not currently accepting new Czech Republic customers, limiting its direct competitive relevance to a reference model. Card subscription visibility or control appears in none of the three claims and is absent from each participant's confirmed product scope. This bears on H4: the participants that claim the strongest process credentials for recurring payment management leave card subscriptions entirely outside their differentiation narrative, which is the structural asymmetry H4 identifies as unknown to clients before they make contact.

**[rare]** One of 11 participants claims a differentiator that no product feature can replicate and that is independent of any technology investment. substitute:contact-centre-call's primary claim is the customer's absolute and unconditional legal right to cancel any Continuous Payment Authority by contacting the bank; the bank must process this request regardless of the merchant's position, and the customer does not need to resolve the underlying dispute before the bank acts. No other participant in this cohort makes a claim of this structure, because the right belongs to the banking relationship under payment law rather than to a feature any team built. Evidence rests on regulatory sources (FCA published guidance, GoCardless published guidance; S-sub-fca, S-sub-gocardless) rather than a primary publisher with a product to document; this is the honest ceiling for a substitute participant with no publishing organisation behind it. This bears directly on H2: the contact centre is the only channel in this cohort where the legal floor for stopping a card-on-file subscription is actually enforced, which is the same gap H2 names. The same card's confirmed limitation is that the channel is reactive, requires the customer to discover the charge before calling, and carries wait-time friction that secondary sources identify as a primary driver of migration to neobank products.
