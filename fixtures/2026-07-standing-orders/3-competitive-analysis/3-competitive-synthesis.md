# Competitive Synthesis -- Standing Order and Recurring Commitment Management

The Czech recurring commitment management market has built thorough self-service infrastructure for standing orders and then stopped at that boundary. Card subscription visibility and card-side payment control exist in four participants in this cohort, none of them Czech-market-primary; the infrastructure enabling those capabilities at a Czech bank is already deployed at Ceska sporitelna's parent group and embedded in the one Czech-licensed neobank competitor that has moved first. H1, H2 and H4 have market answers, but those answers are outside Czech retail banking reach. H3 has a partial market answer: the bank can stop a payment mandate but cannot cancel the underlying merchant contract, and every participant in this cohort that has addressed H2 confirms that same ceiling. H5, H6 and H8 have no market answer in this cohort.

[rec:R-A7-01]

## What the market offers

Eleven participants describe recurring commitment management in two vocabularies: the bank's operational vocabulary (standing orders, direct debits, continuous payment authorities) and the client's commercial vocabulary (subscriptions, what they are paying for). Czech incumbents use the first exclusively; the two UK incumbents with dedicated subscription products and the neobanks use the second at least in product naming. The difference in vocabulary corresponds to a difference in what the participant offers to manage and how far its control reaches toward the client's actual intent.

**[universal] Standing order lifecycle management is the near-universal digital self-service baseline, present in 9 of 11 participants.** The six participants with confirmed digital self-service are kb.cz (digital-only since January 2024, free at all customer types), airbank.cz (create, edit, pause and cancel documented in step-by-step help pages), revolut.com (for accounts holding a European IBAN), n26.com (in-app, step-by-step documented in a dedicated support article), lloydsbank.com (online and mobile banking, up to 25,000 GBP via mobile) and natwest.com (app and online banking, two working days' notice required before the next payment date). csob.cz claims standing order management in the SmartBanking portal; the primary portal returned HTTP 500 across two research rounds, leaving the self-service flow unconfirmable. moneta.cz confirms standing orders are available via its internet banking platform but the lifecycle detail is not documented in available sources. substitute:contact-centre-call provides agent-mediated standing order cancellation, effective within 48 hours under UK and EU payment rules. Outside this count: tapix.io (a B2B API vendor with no consumer product) and monzo.com (UK-only, offering ring-fencing of money for standing orders and direct debits rather than standing order lifecycle management). All nine frame the offering in the bank's operational vocabulary: "trvalý príkaz" in Czech participants, "standing order" in UK ones. The offering covers bank-initiated credit transfers only; card-based recurring charges are outside its scope across every participant. This bears on H1 and H4: standing order management is the capability the market has built out in digital self-service, while card subscription management remains largely unaddressed at the self-service layer, and the vocabulary difference maps directly to the asymmetric client experience H4 describes.

**[emerging] A dedicated subscription visibility product, named in the client's commercial vocabulary, appears in 4 of 11 participants.** revolut.com offers Smart Subscriptions for Czech customers, claiming to display subscriptions, direct debits and recurring payments in one view, with notifications when a payment is due or funds are insufficient, and controls to block a specific amount or merchant. lloydsbank.com offers Manage Subscriptions, launched in partnership with Minna Technologies in July 2020, surfacing a snapshot of all debit card subscriptions with block, unblock and cancellation initiation accessible via the three-dot menu in the app. natwest.com offers a Subscriptions feature covering recurring debit card payments from supported merchants, with individual payment amounts, next payment dates and 12-month spending history, accessible under Payments > Regular Payments > Subscriptions. tapix.io offers a white-label Subscription Management UI module for banks, covering card subscriptions, pattern-based recurring transactions, standing orders and direct debits through a single API rather than separating by payment type. The three consumer-facing participants (revolut.com, lloydsbank.com, natwest.com) use "subscriptions" in their product name, framing the offering in what the client pays for rather than the payment mechanism the bank processes. No Czech incumbent in this cohort has a named equivalent. This bears on H1: the market has begun constructing the consolidated recurring-payment view the problem describes, but only at three consumer-facing participants, none of them Czech incumbents.

**[emerging] Five of 11 participants offer a mechanism to stop or block a card subscription, and all five frame the offering as bank-side payment control rather than cancellation of the commercial contract with the merchant.** revolut.com blocks a specific merchant or subscription amount from the Smart Subscriptions view but states explicitly that blocking does not cancel the subscription and that cancellation requires contacting the merchant directly. lloydsbank.com allows customers to initiate subscription cancellation from the app but customers must contact the service provider to terminate the underlying contract; the card confirms that once cancellation is initiated in-app it cannot be reversed without calling the bank. natwest.com's Subscriptions feature connects customers to the merchant's own cancellation page or app rather than cancelling the payment mandate from the bank side; the card states the feature "does not end the underlying contract." monzo.com provides virtual card deletion as a mechanism to cut off a recurring charge tied to that card credential (the card number ceases to exist, ending any subscription billed to it), and separately provides in-app mandate cancellation for direct debit-based recurring payments, which does cancel the mandate at the bank level rather than merely blocking it. substitute:contact-centre-call holds an unconditional legal right under UK and EU payment law to cancel any Continuous Payment Authority on a customer's instruction, stopping the bank's payment to the merchant; the card confirms that this stops the payment but does not cancel the subscription contract. Czech incumbents csob.cz, kb.cz, airbank.cz and moneta.cz have no documented mechanism for card subscription stopping; n26.com has none confirmed. Across all five, the offering is control over the bank's payment instruction rather than resolution of the commercial relationship with the merchant. This bears on H2 (no mechanism to stop a card subscription at the CS bank): the market holds mechanisms, but every mechanism in this cohort is positioned as payment blocking or credential removal rather than subscription termination.

**[universal] Recurring commitment management is offered as a set of separate, payment-type-specific interfaces by 9 of 11 participants; no participant in this cohort delivers a confirmed unified view across all three commitment types in a single interface.** lloydsbank.com and natwest.com are the clearest examples: both provide a subscription management section (covering debit card subscriptions only), a standing order section and a direct debit section as distinct locations within the same app, with no unified recurring commitment dashboard spanning all three, confirmed explicitly in both cards. Czech incumbents csob.cz, kb.cz, airbank.cz and moneta.cz offer standing order management only, with no subscription or direct debit management layer in any confirmed digital channel. n26.com offers standing orders and named Spaces sub-accounts for ring-fencing but no subscription layer. monzo.com provides a direct debit cancellation list, a Bills Pot for ring-fencing committed spending and virtual cards for subscription credential control: three separate mechanisms with no single entry point and no shared interface. substitute:contact-centre-call provides no consolidated view; agents access the relevant system by the commitment type the customer is calling about. tapix.io is the sole participant whose API covers all recurring payment types in one integration, but it is a B2B vendor with no consumer product. revolut.com's Smart Subscriptions is the nearest consumer-facing approach to a cross-type display, claiming to include subscriptions, direct debits and recurring payments in one view; whether Czech-account standing orders appear in the same interface is not confirmed in the sources available for this run. This bears on H1 and H4: the market's architecture reflects the same type-based siloing that H1 and H4 name as the client's problem, and no participant in this cohort confirms having closed the gap at the consumer layer.

**[rare] One of 11 participants, tapix.io, is a pure B2B infrastructure vendor whose product is the cross-type recurring payment intelligence that no consumer-facing participant in this cohort confirms delivering.** 1 of 11. Tapix's Recurring Payments Intelligence API processes approximately 1.5 billion transactions per month, covering card subscriptions, pattern-based recurring transactions, standing orders and direct debits through a single integration, with stated accuracy of 99.99% at approximately 3-millisecond processing latency. Its white-label Subscription Management UI module is embedded in bank apps without the Tapix brand visible to consumers. Confirmed clients include Revolut, bunq, Raiffeisenbank and UniCredit Bank; OTP Bank selected Tapix as its sole transaction enrichment provider in September 2025; Tapix powers subscription management for 73 million users globally. Tapix is a Czech company (Dateio s.r.o., Prague) and received the FinTech of the Year 2025 award from the Czech Fintech Association. Its cross-type recurring payment intelligence is a commercially deployed capability in this market, not a hypothetical build target. This bears on H1 and H2: the capability to identify and surface all commitment types in one view, and to initiate card subscription blocking across payment types, exists as a vendored product that participants in this cohort have already deployed at scale.

## Who the market serves

The eleven cards describe a market that has drawn its target segment along two fault lines: geography and commitment type. Czech-market retail participants serve the traditional standing-order customer and stop there; the participants with the most capable subscription management tools are concentrated in the UK and are unavailable to Czech retail banking customers. The one participant with a unified view across all commitment types operates as B2B infrastructure rather than a consumer proposition.

**[emerging]** Czech-market retail participants serve the traditional standing-order customer and exclude the card-subscription customer. CSOB, KB, Air Bank, and MONETA (4 of 11) each confirm standing order management as a core capability and explicitly confirm no card subscription management, no recurring card payment visibility, and no subscription detection in their consumer-facing products. KB's FAQ covers standing orders, SIPO, and direct debit registration but addresses none of these for card-based recurring charges. Air Bank's confirmed-not-to-do states that standing order scope covers bank-initiated credit transfers only, not card-based recurring charges; CSOB and MONETA carry the same exclusion across their respective confirmed-not-to-do sections. This structural boundary is what underlies H1 (no unified view is possible if card subscriptions are outside the standing-order channel's scope) and H2 (no mechanism to stop card subscriptions exists at the participants serving the majority of Czech retail customers).

**[emerging]** The participants with the most capable subscription management are geographically unavailable to Czech customers. Monzo (UK only), Lloyds Bank (UK only, England and Wales), NatWest (UK only), and N26 (not currently accepting new Czech customers) account for 4 of 11 participants. Among them, Monzo offers in-app direct debit cancellation and virtual card controls; Lloyds embeds three-click subscription cancellation via its Minna Technologies partnership; NatWest provides a subscriptions view with 12-month payment history; and N26 supports full standing order lifecycle management in-app. None of these capabilities is accessible to a new Czech retail banking customer. Revolut, holding a Czech banking licence and offering Smart Subscriptions to Czech customers, is the single participant that brings subscription management into the Czech market. The capabilities the market has produced for the problems H1 and H2 name are deployed outside Czech reach.

**[emerging]** Participants with subscription management explicitly serve the customer who wants to block a payment, not the customer who wants to cancel the underlying contract. Revolut states that blocking a payment does not cancel the subscription and that cancellation requires contacting the merchant directly. Lloyds states that customers must contact the service provider to terminate the contract after the app blocks further payments. NatWest states that stopping a payment via its Subscriptions feature does not end the underlying contract. The substitute:contact-centre-call confirms that cancelling a recurring payment via the bank does not cancel the underlying obligation. Four of 11 participants draw this distinction explicitly, and no participant claims to serve the customer who wants contract cancellation completed from inside the banking product. This defines the scope of what an H2 resolution can commit to: the bank's capability stops the payment mandate; the contractual relationship between client and merchant remains outside the banking channel at every participant that addresses this.

**[rare]** Only one participant explicitly serves customers who cannot use digital banking for recurring payment management. The substitute:contact-centre-call (1 of 11) requires no app, no online banking registration, and no digital literacy; its card names customers without smartphones, older customers, and customers in low-connectivity areas as the population no other participant reaches. The direction of travel among Czech-market incumbents reinforces this gap: KB eliminated paper standing order submission at branches as of January 2024, making its standing order channel digital-only; Air Bank and MONETA are positioned as digital-first; Revolut is neobank-only. The substitute channel's confirmed-not-to-do includes proactive visibility, meaning the non-digital customer receives reactive payment control only, not the consolidated view H1 requires or the consistent cross-type experience H4 names.

**[rare]** One participant in this cohort serves the segment requiring a unified view of all three commitment types, but exclusively as a B2B proposition to banks. Tapix (1 of 11) provides an API covering card subscriptions, pattern-based recurring transactions, standing orders, and direct debits in a single enrichment layer, described in its product materials as having no single-network blind spot. No consumer-facing participant in this cohort surfaces all three commitment types in a single view: Lloyds and NatWest each explicitly confirm that their subscription management covers debit card subscriptions only and that direct debits and standing orders are managed in separate app sections. Revolut's Smart Subscriptions covers subscriptions, direct debits, and recurring payments together but treats standing orders as a separate feature. The customer H1 describes (no single view of all three commitment types) is not served by any participant as a direct consumer product; the technical infrastructure to enable it exists in the market but operates exclusively at the B2B layer.

## What the products actually do

The eleven cards show a market divided by commitment type at every ring. Standing order management is consistently self-service digital. Card-on-file subscription management is confined to four participants outside the Czech market. No participant, including those that have built the most capable subscription features, surfaces all three commitment types in one view. The controls that exist block charges; they do not end merchant contracts.

**[common] Digital self-service standing order management is the consistent floor across retail banking participants.** Eight of 11 participants offer customers a channel to create, modify and cancel standing orders without calling the bank or visiting a branch: csob.cz (web portal), kb.cz (digital-only since January 2024), airbank.cz (full create-edit-pause-cancel lifecycle documented with step-by-step guides in the app and internet banking), moneta.cz (internet banking platform), revolut.com (EU IBAN accounts), n26.com (full lifecycle in the mobile app), lloydsbank.com and natwest.com (both via mobile app and online banking with a documented create-modify-cancel lifecycle). The substitute:contact-centre-call is phone-mediated rather than digital self-service; tapix.io is B2B infrastructure with no consumer product. Standing order management is the one commitment type where every Czech incumbent in this cohort has confirmed a digital path; the gap this run examines begins where standing orders end. This finding bears on H4: the contrast between the standing order capability and the absence of any equivalent for card-on-file subscriptions is the sharpest structural expression of how capability differs by type.

**[universal] Commitment types are managed in separate sections across every consumer-facing participant; no product surfaces standing orders, direct debits and card-on-file subscriptions in a single unified view.** Ten of 11 participants split the three types or exclude one type entirely. lloydsbank.com explicitly confirms its subscription management covers debit card subscriptions only, not standing orders or direct debits in the same view. natwest.com explicitly confirms direct debit mandates and standing orders are managed separately from its Subscriptions feature. revolut.com's Smart Subscriptions groups subscriptions, direct debits and recurring payments together but standing order management is confirmed separate, and the full scope of the Czech interface was not confirmable from primary sources. All four Czech incumbents (csob.cz, kb.cz, airbank.cz, moneta.cz) either treat card subscriptions as outside scope or do not surface them at all; standing orders and direct debits are accessed through separate paths. The substitute:contact-centre-call offers no dashboard of any kind. tapix.io is the only participant whose data architecture covers all three types without separation, but it has no consumer product and no confirmed Czech bank deployment. This is the market-level expression of H1 and the structural basis of H4: the commitment-type split is not a peculiarity of any one bank; it is what the market has built.

**[emerging] In-app visibility of card-on-file subscription charges as a named product feature is present in 4 of 11 participants, all outside the Czech market.** revolut.com (Smart Subscriptions: subscriptions, direct debits and recurring payments in one list with merchant-level blocking and due-date notifications), monzo.com (direct debit mandate list with in-app cancellation; virtual card auto-delete as a supplementary subscription control mechanism), lloydsbank.com (Manage Subscriptions via Minna Technologies and Visa partnership since July 2020: three-click cancellation, real-time price-change notifications) and natwest.com (Subscriptions tab showing recurring debit card payments for the past 12 months with next payment dates and 12-month spend history per subscription) each surface recurring card charges as an addressable list. All four Czech incumbents confirmed they do not offer this; n26.com has a comprehensive standing order feature but explicitly no card subscription management. This finding aligns with trend T2. It bears on H1 (card subscription visibility is the missing element from any unified view), H2 (in-app visibility is a precondition for in-app control) and H4 (the gap between the standing order and card subscription capabilities is what H4 describes at the product level).

**[emerging] Wherever in-app card payment controls exist, they block the charge but cannot cancel the underlying merchant contract; 5 of 11 participants confirm this boundary.** revolut.com states that blocking a subscription or merchant does not cancel the subscription and that customers must contact the merchant directly. lloydsbank.com confirms that its in-app cancellation blocks further payments but customers must contact the service provider to terminate the contract. natwest.com confirms that stopping a payment through the Subscriptions feature does not end the contract. monzo.com's virtual card auto-delete prevents future charges without ending the merchant subscription. The substitute:contact-centre-call confirms the same boundary: cancelling a Continuous Payment Authority at the bank stops future payments but does not settle the merchant-side obligation, and the FTC recorded nearly 70 consumer complaints per day in 2024 about recurring subscription cancellation difficulties, a portion driven by this gap. This finding aligns with trend R3, which identifies the structural regulatory asymmetry between card-on-file subscriptions and SEPA direct debits: the bank can revoke a direct debit mandate because it participates in the mandate; it cannot revoke a card-on-file agreement because that agreement sits with the merchant. This bears on H2 (the bank's inability to fully stop a card subscription is not a build choice by any participant in this cohort; it is the outer boundary of what bank-side card payment controls can reach) and H4 (the bank can cancel a standing order outright and cancel a direct debit mandate, but can only block a card-on-file subscription payment).

**[emerging] Per-subscription virtual card mechanisms for payment isolation at the card-number level are offered by 2 of 11 participants.** revolut.com provides both multi-use virtual cards (permanent details, suitable for recurring subscriptions) and disposable cards (single-transaction, auto-deleted after use), with Czech users holding up to 20 active virtual cards. monzo.com (Extra tier, GBP 3 per month and above) offers virtual cards that auto-delete on a customer-set date, allowing a card created for a free trial to expire before the trial converts to a paid charge without requiring any action at cancellation time. Both approaches isolate a subscription at the card-number level without affecting other payments and without requiring merchant contact to block future charges. This finding aligns with trend T3. It bears on H2: these mechanisms provide a distinct path to stopping a card-on-file charge that differs from the merchant-level blocking described in the previous finding, though neither approach cancels the underlying contract.

**[rare] B2B infrastructure for automated detection and classification of recurring payments across all three commitment types is represented by 1 of 11 participants.** tapix.io processes approximately 1.5 billion transactions per month, identifies card subscriptions, standing orders, direct debits and pattern-based recurring charges through a single API without relying on card scheme data fields, and provides a white-label Subscription Management UI that bank partners embed in their own apps with no Tapix branding visible to consumers. The data model carries no single-type blind spot. Confirmed production clients in the cards include revolut.com, Raiffeisenbank and UniCredit Bank; the Erste Group, parent of the brief's principal, is also a confirmed tapix.io client. No Czech retail bank partnership is publicly confirmed, and OTP Bank, the most recently named deployment, is Hungarian rather than Czech. This finding aligns with trend T1. It bears on H1: the market contains a commercially deployed technology layer whose data model covers all three commitment types in one structure, and this layer already powers subscription visibility for at least one participant in this cohort.

## What they claim sets them apart

The cards show USP claims in three main patterns: consumer-facing tools for subscription or direct debit visibility and control, digital-first process leadership for standing orders, and institutional scale or penetration. Two participants fall outside all three: tapix.io as a B2B infrastructure layer and substitute:contact-centre-call as a legally grounded channel rather than a product. No claim of a unified recurring commitment view confirmed from primary documentation appears anywhere in the cohort.

**[universal]** No participant offers a confirmed unified recurring commitment view spanning standing orders, direct debits and card subscriptions in a single consumer screen, and the only participant that approaches the claim cannot have it verified. 10 of 11 participants either make no such claim or explicitly list its absence in their confirmed scope. revolut.com's Smart Subscriptions is described as "full visibility of all subscriptions, direct debits and recurring payments in one place," but revolut.com and help.revolut.com returned HTTP 403 on all access attempts during research, making the detailed scope unconfirmable from Revolut's own documentation. lloydsbank.com and natwest.com each confirm in their own stated scope that their subscription features cover debit card payments only and that standing orders and direct debits are managed separately, with no unified view across all payment types. The remaining 8 of 11 make no unified view claim of any kind. This finding is the market expression of H1: a product that resolves the gap CS identified is not available in a confirmed form from any participant in this cohort.

**[common]** Eight of 11 participants claim customer-facing control over at least one recurring payment type as a differentiator, but each addresses a different commitment type and the claims do not add up to coverage of all three. kb.cz and airbank.cz claim management of standing orders (bank-initiated credit transfers). revolut.com, lloydsbank.com and natwest.com claim visibility and payment control for card subscriptions. monzo.com claims in-app cancellation of direct debit mandates (UK BACS only) and proactive card controls via virtual cards with auto-delete. n26.com claims standing order lifecycle management entirely in the mobile app. substitute:contact-centre-call claims the legal right to cancel any Continuous Payment Authority on the customer's behalf. csob.cz (no recurring payment management feature confirmed across two research runs), moneta.cz (USP claims are digital penetration and real-time personalization, not payment control), and tapix.io (B2B only, no consumer-facing product) fall outside this group. The fragmentation bears on H1 (no unified view across all commitment types) and H4 (process and capability differ by commitment type, with clients not told which applies before they make contact).

**[emerging]** Three of 11 participants name subscription management as a dedicated consumer product, but all three confirm the same ceiling: they stop at payment blocking or merchant signposting for card subscriptions, not contract cancellation. revolut.com's Smart Subscriptions allows the customer to block a specific merchant or subscription amount; it does not cancel the underlying subscription mandate, and the cards state that cancellation requires contacting the merchant directly. lloydsbank.com's Manage Subscriptions (built with Minna Technologies since July 2020) initiates a cancellation flow but requires the customer to contact the service provider to terminate the contract; the card confirms that the app "blocks further payments" while directing customers elsewhere, and that a cancellation once initiated in the app cannot be reversed without calling the bank. natwest.com's Subscriptions feature links customers to merchant cancellation pages rather than cancelling the payment from the bank side, and natwest.com's own feature description states explicitly that stopping a payment does not end the underlying contract. The evidence basis for revolut.com and lloydsbank.com is reduced: their primary documentation was inaccessible during research (HTTP 403 and HTTP errors respectively), and their scope is drawn from secondary sources. natwest.com's feature is confirmed from primary documentation (S-natwest-sub-page). All three are either unavailable in or not operating primarily in the Czech Republic: Revolut holds a Czech banking licence but primary confirmation of Smart Subscriptions' scope is absent; Lloyds and NatWest are UK-only. This bearing on H2 is direct: the product category that has emerged to address card subscription control does not resolve the gap H2 names, because blocking a payment and cancelling a card-on-file authority are distinct acts in every deployment confirmed in this cohort.

**[emerging]** Three of 11 participants claim standing order digital-first process or documentation leadership as their primary differentiator, and none of the three makes any card subscription management claim alongside it. kb.cz was the first Czech incumbent to confirm a digital-only standing order channel, eliminating paper instructions at branches as of 1 January 2024, confirmed in KB's own customer communications (S-kb-cards-page, S-kb-sipo-faq); its FAQ is the most explicit documentation of recurring payment types among Czech incumbents, covering standing orders, SIPO and direct debit in a single resource. airbank.cz claims the most detailed standing order self-service guide among Czech incumbents in this cohort, with a documented create-edit-pause-cancel lifecycle and step-by-step screenshots in its own help pages, independently rated by 5nej.cz and pickthebank.eu for ease of recurring payment management (S-airbank-trvaly-help, S-airbank-ib-standing, S-airbank-5nej, S-airbank-pickthebank). n26.com claims the most explicitly documented standing order lifecycle among the neobanks in this cohort, with a dedicated support article covering creation, editing and deletion from primary documentation (S-n26-standing-orders); N26 is not currently accepting new Czech Republic customers, limiting its direct competitive relevance to a reference model. Card subscription visibility or control appears in none of the three claims and is absent from each participant's confirmed product scope. This bears on H4: the participants that claim the strongest process credentials for recurring payment management leave card subscriptions entirely outside their differentiation narrative, which is the structural asymmetry H4 identifies as unknown to clients before they make contact.

**[rare]** One of 11 participants claims a differentiator that no product feature can replicate and that is independent of any technology investment. substitute:contact-centre-call's primary claim is the customer's absolute and unconditional legal right to cancel any Continuous Payment Authority by contacting the bank; the bank must process this request regardless of the merchant's position, and the customer does not need to resolve the underlying dispute before the bank acts. No other participant in this cohort makes a claim of this structure, because the right belongs to the banking relationship under payment law rather than to a feature any team built. Evidence rests on regulatory sources (FCA published guidance, GoCardless published guidance; S-sub-fca, S-sub-gocardless) rather than a primary publisher with a product to document; this is the honest ceiling for a substitute participant with no publishing organisation behind it. This bears directly on H2: the contact centre is the only channel in this cohort where the legal floor for stopping a card-on-file subscription is actually enforced, which is the same gap H2 names. The same card's confirmed limitation is that the channel is reactive, requires the customer to discover the charge before calling, and carries wait-time friction that secondary sources identify as a primary driver of migration to neobank products.

## What it costs

The cards show a market where recurring commitment management carries no per-action monetary charge at every participant that publishes pricing. The more significant market fact is that five of eleven participants publish nothing specific about what commitment management costs, including two Czech incumbents whose primary sites were inaccessible during research. Where a fee structure exists, it is always account-tier based rather than action-based.

**[common] Six of eleven participants publish confirmed pricing for recurring commitment management, and every one of them confirms zero marginal cost per action.** KB states that standing order setup, modification and cancellation are free for all customers. Air Bank includes standing order management at no additional cost alongside a free current account. N26 includes standing orders across Standard (free), You (EUR 9.90/month) and Metal (EUR 16.90/month) tiers at no additional charge. Lloyds Bank confirms both standing order management and subscription management are included at no additional charge across all account tiers. NatWest confirms subscription and standing order management are included in all tiers at no additional charge. Monzo includes direct debit cancellation from the in-app list on its free Basic tier. This is 6 of 11. This finding does not directly bear on H1, H2 or H4; it establishes that pricing is not the barrier the brief describes. The problems the cards surface are capability and information gaps, not monetary access barriers.

**[emerging] Five of eleven participants publish no confirmed consumer pricing for recurring commitment management.** CSOB's primary site returned HTTP 500 across both this run and the prior one; its standing order fee structure is not confirmable from any accessible source. MONETA's standing order management fee was not confirmed in any source accessed. Revolut's tier names are known (Standard free, Plus, Premium, Metal, Ultra) but which tier includes Smart Subscriptions was not confirmable from accessible sources after HTTP 403 on all Revolut primary properties. Tapix publishes no consumer pricing by design and operates on a per-API-call B2B contract disclosed only in commercial engagement. The substitute:contact-centre-call participant has no pricing entity by definition. This is 5 of 11. The two Czech incumbents in this group, CSOB and MONETA, are the same participants whose capability scope could not be confirmed; the pricing gap is a dimension of H4, since a client who cannot determine what the bank can do by commitment type cannot determine what each type costs either.

**[rare] One participant, Monzo, gates its most capable subscription control mechanism behind a paid account tier.** Virtual cards with auto-delete, the mechanism that allows stopping a recurring card payment without merchant contact and without blocking the entire card, are available on Extra (GBP 3/month) and above; direct debit cancellation from the in-app list remains on the free Basic tier. This is 1 of 11. Revolut carries a tiered pricing structure and Smart Subscriptions is confirmed available in the Czech market, but the tier that grants access was not confirmable from accessible sources and may bring the count to 2 of 11. This finding bears on H2: the mechanism in this cohort that most directly addresses the bank's inability to stop a card subscription without blocking the whole card carries a monthly monetary cost in the one non-Czech market where it exists.

**[emerging] Two of eleven participants, Lloyds Bank and NatWest, explicitly confirm that subscription management is included at no additional charge at every account tier, including the free tier.** Lloyds added the Minna Technologies subscription management feature in July 2020 and attached no fee at any tier. NatWest's in-app Subscriptions feature is free across Select (no monthly fee), Reward (GBP 2/month) and higher tiers. This is 2 of 11. No participant that has confirmed a subscription management capability charges a premium for it, making zero-cost subscription visibility the pricing precedent set by the incumbents who have moved first. This finding does not directly bear on H1, H2 or H4; it establishes that the two UK banks with the closest comparator capability have already set the client expectation of no additional price.

## Where they differ from each other

The cards reveal a market divided less cleanly than the Czech versus neobank label implies. N26, a neobank, sits on the same capability side as the four Czech banks for the problems this run is investigating, while Lloyds and NatWest, two traditional incumbents, have card subscription management that the Czech banks do not. The real dividing line in this cohort tracks regulatory market context and individual product strategy rather than institution type.

**[universal]** Standing order self-service in a digital or human-mediated channel is near-universal and is not where these participants separate. csob.cz (web portal; primary portal unreachable across both research rounds), kb.cz (digital-only since January 2024, paper channel eliminated), airbank.cz (create-edit-pause-cancel lifecycle with step-by-step screenshots), moneta.cz (payment templates for recurring transactions, standing orders via internet banking), revolut.com (for EU IBAN accounts), monzo.com (via Bills Pot and direct debit management), n26.com (full lifecycle in-app only), lloydsbank.com (online and mobile, with a GBP 25,000 mobile limit), natwest.com (online and mobile, two working days notice required), and substitute:contact-centre-call (agent-mediated, effective within 48 hours) all offer this. 10 of 11; tapix.io is infrastructure with no consumer-facing product. Quality varies: airbank.cz is the only Czech participant with a documented pause capability; csob.cz's standing order scope is unconfirmable from primary sources; moneta.cz's lifecycle beyond "payment templates" is unconfirmed. None of that variation changes the core finding: standing order self-service does not separate any participant from its peers. What creates separation is whether any of the other two commitment types receive the same treatment, which is what H4 is about.

**[emerging]** Card subscription visibility or control of any form is present in 4 of 11 participants and is confirmed absent from all four Czech market participants in this cohort. revolut.com provides a dedicated Smart Subscriptions view of all subscriptions, direct debits, and recurring payments, with per-merchant block controls, available to Czech account holders. monzo.com offers virtual cards with configurable auto-delete dates, allowing a customer to end a recurring card charge by letting the card expire rather than acting at cancellation time. lloydsbank.com embeds block and cancel initiation under Manage Subscriptions, built in partnership with Minna Technologies since July 2020. natwest.com surfaces 12 months of recurring debit card payments from supported merchants and links each entry to the merchant's own cancellation page. 4 of 11. csob.cz, kb.cz, airbank.cz, and moneta.cz each confirm the absence of subscription detection, recurring card payment management, or merchant-level card payment control. n26.com also lacks this capability despite being a neobank, and is no longer accepting new Czech customers. The dividing variable is not neobank versus incumbent: it is whether the participant operates in a regulatory environment where subscription management has become a competitive or compliance expectation (UK FCA scrutiny of subscription practices; Revolut's global product strategy built around a subscription tier). Czech-market-primary participants have no equivalent pressure visible in the cards. Bears on H2 and H4.

**[universal]** Every consumer-facing participant that offers recurring payment management separates it by payment type, and no participant in this cohort provides a confirmed single view covering standing orders, direct debits, and card-on-file subscriptions simultaneously. lloydsbank.com states this directly: its subscription management covers debit card subscriptions only, with standing orders and direct debits in separate app sections and no unified dashboard. natwest.com confirms the same structure: the Subscriptions feature covers card payments only, and direct debit mandates and standing orders are managed separately. monzo.com separates its direct debit list from the Bills Pot and standing order features. All four Czech banks manage standing orders in a section distinct from card management, with no subscription layer present at all. n26.com manages standing orders in-app but has no subscription layer. revolut.com claims that Smart Subscriptions shows all subscriptions, direct debits, and recurring payments in a single place, but the primary source was inaccessible throughout this research (HTTP 403) and the claim cannot be confirmed or excluded from the cards. Confirmed separation: at least 9 of 11; tapix.io is not applicable as an infrastructure participant. The one plausible exception is the one participant whose primary documentation is unconfirmable. That is the honest position the cards support. Bears on H1: no confirmed participant has resolved the unified commitment view, which means what H1 describes is an absent capability across this entire cohort, not a gap behind a single leader.

**[emerging]** Among participants that offer any form of card subscription management, stopping a future payment does not resolve the underlying merchant contract, and this limitation is consistent across 3 of 11. revolut.com states explicitly that blocking a subscription does not cancel it and that cancellation requires contacting the merchant directly. natwest.com's in-app cancellation initiates a link to the merchant's own cancellation page rather than acting from the bank side; stopping a payment via the feature does not end the underlying contract. lloydsbank.com initiates a cancellation from within the app, but the card confirms that the underlying contract remains the customer's obligation and that once initiated the cancellation cannot be reversed in-app. The contrast with other commitment types is precise: for standing orders (where the bank initiates the payment), all 10 consumer-facing participants can cancel entirely within the bank channel. For BACS direct debits, monzo.com cancels the mandate from the app in a bank-side action that ends collection authority without merchant contact. For card-on-file subscriptions specifically, no participant in this cohort has a mechanism that cancels the authority from the bank side; the digital tools stop at blocking or redirecting. The 4 Czech banks have no card subscription mechanism at all, so the limitation is structural rather than a confirmed design choice. Bears on H2 (the bank cannot stop a card subscription) and H3 (client believes they cancelled but continues to be charged).

**[emerging]** Proactive notification of upcoming or changed subscription charges distinguishes 2 of 11 participants and is absent from all Czech banks in this cohort. revolut.com notifies users when a subscription payment is due or when funds are insufficient to cover it. lloydsbank.com sends push notifications when a subscription price changes and when a payment is due, placing the bank in the customer's awareness before the charge appears on the statement. moneta.cz operates real-time transaction-triggered personalization at scale (a cross-sell mechanism: travel insurance offered within seven seconds of a flight booking), but this is not a proactive commitment notification and is a distinct capability. The remaining 8 participants have no confirmed proactive subscription notification of any form. 2 of 11. The gap bears on H4: a customer whose bank notifies them of a subscription price change encounters the commitment differently from one who discovers the increase on a monthly statement, and that difference is currently confined to one UK incumbent and one global neobank in this cohort, with no Czech-market participant present on the notifying side.

## Verdict

### Diagnosis

The Czech recurring commitment management market has built standing order self-service to near-universal coverage and then split into two cohorts that do not overlap: four Czech incumbents stopped there, while four participants outside Czech reach built subscription visibility and control on top of it, with the B2B infrastructure enabling the second group already deployed at the Ceska sporitelna parent group without a confirmed consumer product at any Czech bank.

### Parity

Two standards are universal across this cohort. Digital self-service standing order lifecycle management (9 of 11) is the capability any Czech banking participant must have to avoid being visibly deficient. Payment-type-separated management with no unified view (10 of 11) is the architectural norm: every participant in this cohort separates commitment types, so inheriting parity means inheriting the gap H1 names. Meeting both universal standards places a participant at the market floor, not ahead of it. The common standard -- customer-facing control over at least one recurring payment type as a differentiator (8 of 11) -- adds fragmented commitment-type coverage to that floor but does not close the gap.

### The opening

H5, H6 and H8 meet no market response in this cohort. The emerging tier shows that H1, H2 and H4 are being addressed outside Czech reach: in-app card subscription visibility at 4 of 11 participants (all non-Czech), virtual card isolation at 2 of 11, proactive subscription notification at 2 of 11. H3 is addressed partially -- every participant that has tried to close H2 confirms the boundary the features facet establishes: the bank can stop a payment mandate but cannot cancel the merchant contract, because card-on-file agreements sit with the merchant and not with the bank. The convergence of H5, H6 and H8's complete market absence with the emerging tier's geographic concentration in the UK and one Czech-licensed neobank gives stage 4 two distinct inputs: an established problem space (H1, H2, H3, H4) where market answers exist and the technology enabling them is accessible via the B2B layer, and an unaddressed problem space (H5, H6, H8) where no participant in this cohort has moved.

### Risk

Revolut holds a Czech banking licence and has Smart Subscriptions available to Czech customers with merchant-level blocking and due-date notifications; it is already operating in this market with the capability the brief describes, and Czech users can hold up to 20 active virtual cards for subscription isolation. The structural ceiling the features and differentiation facets establish is the second risk: every participant in this cohort that has built in-app card subscription control confirms that bank-side card payment controls can stop a payment mandate but cannot cancel the underlying merchant contract. The features facet traces this to the regulatory asymmetry between card-on-file agreements and SEPA direct debit mandates -- the bank cannot revoke what the merchant holds. This ceiling is not a build choice and it affects every participant in this cohort equally; no initiative that stays within the bank channel can resolve H2 entirely. The third risk is the substitute: substitute:contact-centre-call's card confirms that 46% of UK consumers still call first when they have a real problem, that the FTC recorded nearly 70 consumer complaints per day in 2024 about recurring subscription cancellation difficulties, and that wait-time friction is a confirmed driver of migration to neobank products -- which is to say that the friction the initiative is designed to reduce is already pushing customers toward the competitor that has already moved.

## What we could not establish

**The scope of revolut.com's Smart Subscriptions for Czech customers.** All Revolut primary web properties (revolut.com, help.revolut.com, blog.revolut.com) returned HTTP 403 throughout research. Smart Subscriptions is confirmed as available to Czech customers from search result snippets but its detailed scope -- which tier, whether Czech-account standing orders appear in the same view as subscriptions and direct debits, and how cancellation flows -- is not confirmable from primary documentation. This is the one participant in this cohort whose claim to a unified view cannot be excluded from the cards.

**Whether H5, H6 or H8 have market responses not visible in this card set.** The eleven participants were selected to represent trends across four geographic rings; a problem the market has not yet addressed may have answers among participants not profiled, or none at all. The synthesis works only from the cards and makes no claim about the market beyond what the cards confirm.

**The standing order self-service scope and fee structure of csob.cz.** The primary portal (online.csob.cz) returned HTTP 500 across two consecutive discovery runs. CSOB's digital channel for standing order management is claimed but unconfirmable; its subscription management scope is unconfirmable for the same reason.
