## A2-item-1 — Interview, Customer 1, retail client

> Fabricated. No such person and no such interview.

**On why she started looking.** She said her salary account felt leaky. Money left it every month that she
could not place, small amounts and one or two larger ones, and she had stopped being able to say from
memory what was a subscription, what was insurance, and what was the rent. She sat down one evening in
George to make a list of everything that goes out on its own.

**On what she could find.** The standing orders were easy. She found them, saw the rent and a transfer to
her savings, and changed the savings one there and then. Then she went looking for the rest and it got
harder. The direct debits were on a different screen and she was not sure the list was complete, because
one she expected, the mobile phone, was not on it. The subscriptions she pays with the card were nowhere.
They were in the transaction history like any other card payment, and the only way to find them was to
scroll and recognize the names.

**On the one she wanted to stop.** She had cancelled a streaming service months ago, or thought she had,
and it was still charging her 199 crowns every month. She looked for it in George to stop it and there was
nothing to stop, because as far as the app was concerned it was just a card payment that kept happening.
She rang the bank.

**On the call.** She was told the bank cannot stop a subscription paid by card. The person was kind about
it, she said, but the answer was that she had to contact the streaming company herself, or the bank could
block her card. She asked what blocking the card would do to the rest and was told it would stop every
card payment, including the two subscriptions she wanted to keep and her transport pass.

**On what she asked for and did not get.** She asked whether there was a single list somewhere of
everything that leaves the account regularly, all of it in one place, so she could go down it and decide.
She was told there was not, that standing orders and direct debits were separate and the card ones were
not really the bank's to show. She said that was the moment she understood the problem was not one
subscription. It was that nobody, including her, could see the whole of it.

**On what she did in the end.** She cancelled nothing that evening. She said she was afraid that if she
started blocking and cancelling she would break something she needed and not notice for a month, because
there was no list to check against. She was still being charged the 199 when the interview took place.

## A2-item-2 — Interview, Tomáš Beneš, contact centre agent, accounts and payments queue

> Fabricated. No such person and no such interview.

**On what arrives.** He said the recurring-payment calls come in two shapes. One is "what is this thing
that keeps charging me," where the client cannot recognize a payment and wants to know what it is. The
other is "make it stop," where they know exactly what it is and want it gone. The second shape is the one
that goes wrong, because whether he can make it stop depends entirely on which of three kinds it is, and
the client has no reason to know there are three kinds.

**On standing orders.** Those he can handle on the line. He can see them, change the amount or the date,
or cancel one while the client waits. He said if every recurring payment were a standing order this queue
would be half the size.

**On direct debits.** He can cancel the mandate or put a limit on it, but he said it is not always the end
of it. If the client also owes the other side money the cancellation stops the pull and not the debt, and
some of the changes clients ask for are not ones he can make from where he sits. Those he sends to the
mandates desk on a form. He said clients do not understand why one recurring payment can be stopped in
thirty seconds and the next one needs a form and a wait.

**On card subscriptions.** Those he can do nothing about, and he said that is the call that goes worst. The
client can see the charge, he can see the charge, and he has to tell them that the bank cannot cancel it.
The arrangement is between them and the merchant, and the only lever the bank has is blocking the card,
which breaks everything else. He said explaining that he can see a payment but cannot stop it is the
sentence he says most often and the one clients believe least.

**On the missing single view.** He said there is no one screen he can open that shows a client everything
recurring against their account. Standing orders are in one place, mandates in another, and the card
subscriptions are not gathered anywhere at all, they are just in the history. When a client asks him to go
through everything that leaves their account, he cannot do it from a list, he has to go screen by screen
and he still cannot show them the card ones.

**On the ones that come back.** The hardest, he said, is the client who cancelled with the merchant, was
charged anyway, and now wants the bank to get the money back. That is a card dispute, a different process
and a different team, and by the time it reaches them the client has already been told three different
things about whose problem it is.

## A2-item-4 — Interview, Martina Kučerová, back-office payments operations, mandates desk

> Fabricated. No such person and no such interview.

**On the three kinds and why they are not alike.** She said the reason this looks like one problem to a
client and is three problems to the bank is that the three kinds live in three systems and the bank's hold
over each is different. A standing order is the bank's own instruction to pay, so the bank can change it or
stop it outright. A direct debit is a permission the client gave a creditor to pull money, so the bank can
withdraw the permission but the arrangement with the creditor is still there. A card subscription the bank
does not hold at all. It is between the cardholder and the merchant over the card scheme, and the bank
sees it only as it arrives.

**On what she can and cannot do with a mandate.** Cancelling a mandate at the bank stops future
collections, she said, and that much is clean. What is not clean is that clients expect cancelling the
mandate to also undo a charge already taken or to settle whatever the direct debit was paying for, and it
does neither. Those are separate, and one of them is not the bank's at all.

**On the card ones.** She said this is the part that is genuinely outside the bank in the channel. The bank
can block the card or reissue it, and in narrow cases lodge a stop with the scheme, but it cannot see the
merchant's arrangement and cannot cancel it. So when the instruction reaches her desk as "cancel this
subscription" and it is a card subscription, there is nothing on her desk to cancel. She sends it back.

**On the absence of one register.** She said there is no place, on her side or the client's, where all
three sit together. The payments engine has the standing orders, the mandate register has the direct
debits, and the card processor has the card subscriptions, and nothing joins them by client. She said if
someone asked her today to produce a list of everything recurring against one account she could not do it
without going to three systems and she would still not be sure the card list was complete.

**On what the forms carry.** The cancellation forms she works carry the one mandate they are about and
nothing else. There is no field telling her whether the same client also has card subscriptions or three
other mandates, so she cannot see, and cannot warn the client about, the rest of the picture while she
processes the one in front of her.

**On what she thinks the real gap is.** She said the individual actions mostly work, each in its own
system. What does not exist is the client ever being able to see all of it at once and act on it in one
place, and that, she said, is not a broken process, it is a missing one.
